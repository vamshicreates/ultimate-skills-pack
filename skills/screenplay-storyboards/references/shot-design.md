# Shot design decisions

Choose coverage from the scene's dramatic turn, physical geography, and edit needs. Establish space when the audience needs it; tighten framing when a decision or emotional change matters. Use a reaction only when the reaction changes the meaning of the preceding line or action. Preserve an intelligible eyeline and screen direction across cuts unless a deliberate disorientation is called for.

An angle must describe the camera's relationship to the subject: eye level, low/high, over shoulder, POV, profile, top down, etc. Pair this with shot size (wide, medium, close, insert) and movement (static, pan, dolly, handheld, etc.) only when motivated. Do not call every dramatic beat a close-up or prescribe a lens number when the effect can be described more clearly.

For dialogue, identify whose perspective controls the beat, how the audience sees the listener, and whether shared framing or singles best serve the subtext. For action, preserve starting and ending positions and provide enough coverage to cut cleanly. For montage, intercut, offscreen dialogue, voiceover, and dream material, distinguish what is visibly on screen from what is heard or only inferred. If an action line is abstract or unfilmable, propose a visual expression and mark it as an interpretation.

Add a concise rationale for exceptional choices (for example, a low angle to make a reversal of power legible). Cinematography should support the script's tone and production scale. Keep alternate approaches optional rather than multiplying mandatory shots.

For a three-panel sheet, choose three consecutive beats that read as a short visual sequence. A wide top panel can establish the change in space or power; the two lower panels can show action and reaction. Keep the same screen direction and character appearance across all three. If the sequence needs only one or two images, leave the sheet shorter rather than inventing filler.
