# Scoring rubric: script-doctor-v1

## Default scorecard

Apply [format-and-length.md](format-and-length.md) before scoring. The same categories and weights cover micro-dramas, shorts, features, pilots, episodes, seasons, and series, but the evidence is judged relative to each unit's intended duration and ambition. A complete two-minute story or complete episode can earn any score in the range; lack of feature-length development is not itself a weakness. Runtime never multiplies or normalizes the total.

Score each assessable category from 0 to 10 in increments of 0.5. The weighting yields a score out of 100:

`total = sum(category_score / 10 * category_weight)`

Round once at the end to the nearest integer, with halves rounded up. Decimals in category scores are editorial shorthand, not measurement precision. Scores of zero mean assessed, fundamental failure in that dimension; missing evidence is `null`, never zero.

| Key | Category | Weight | Questions that guide judgment |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| character | Character and relationships | 15 | Are wants, fears, contradictions, and choices legible? Do actions arise from the person and situation? Are relationships differentiated? Is change, stasis, or refusal meaningful? For ensembles, evaluate the ensemble rather than demanding one protagonist. |
| plot | Plot and conflict | 15 | Do consequences follow from choices and established circumstances? Are obstacles and stakes specific? Does pressure develop? Are coincidences, information, and revelations credible within the work's chosen form? |
| emotion | Emotional effectiveness | 20 | Does the draft establish attachment, anticipation, vulnerability, or another intended response? Are changes earned? Do setups pay off and consequences persist? Evaluate the intended emotional register, including unease, delight, recognition, or restraint. |
| structure | Structure and pacing | 15 | Do scenes and sequences contribute to the whole? Is information placed effectively? Is repetition productive? Does duration serve the intended experience? Are major transitions and the ending proportionate and prepared? |
| dialogue | Dialogue and subtext | 10 | Are voices specific? Do speech, silence, and behavior express intention and relationships? Is exposition motivated? Do rhythm, register, dialect, humor, or stylization serve the work? Sparse dialogue is not a flaw. |
| theme | Theme and meaning | 10 | Do choices and consequences explore a meaningful question or perspective? Is complexity sustained? Is thematic ambiguity intentional and supported? An explicit moral or speech is not required. |
| cinematic | Cinematic storytelling | 10 | Can action, behavior, sound, space, and images carry the story? Are key moments legible on screen? Are visual or sonic patterns purposeful? Do not penalize low budgets, chamber settings, or an economical style. |
| specificity | Originality and specificity | 5 | Does the work have a particular world, perspective, or execution? Are familiar elements transformed through specific choices? Familiar genre conventions can be executed freshly; novelty alone is not quality. |

## Shared anchors

Apply anchors within each category; avoid averaging vague impressions of the whole script into every row.

| Score | Working interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Fundamental execution problems prevent the intended function; cite the breakdown. |
| 3–4 | Some workable elements, but recurring or substantial weaknesses undermine the function. |
| 5–6 | The function is substantially present, with uneven, predictable, or underdeveloped execution. |
| 7–8 | Effective and sustained execution, supported by specific choices; remaining issues are identifiable. |
| 9–10 | Exceptionally controlled, distinctive execution with few meaningful weaknesses in this dimension. |

Do not grade to a distribution, force a “winner,” reserve high marks for known classics, or inflate scores to be encouraging. A strong category can coexist with a weak one. One structural flaw may affect multiple dimensions, but explain the distinct effects; do not repeatedly apply an arbitrary deduction for the same complaint.

## Interpretation and comparability

- Assess the actual draft. Do not give it points for the film it could become after a rewrite or for performances imagined to rescue it.
- Use this common weighting, with explicit format, unit, and runtime context. Default to comparable groups by form, duration, and unit; show a mixed-format overall ranking only when requested. Different groups' first-place ranks are independent. Discuss genre- and format-specific success within category rationales. If customized rubrics have different weights, produce separate scoring runs; do not mix their totals into a single league table.
- Custom weights must include the same eight keys, be nonnegative, and sum to 100. Set a new rubric ID and record the change before assessment. A positive-weight missing category prevents a complete total. A zero-weight category can still receive qualitative discussion.
- Distinguish `high`, `medium`, and `low` confidence for each category: strong textual support and reliable understanding; plausible but with meaningful interpretive uncertainty; or limited linguistic, cultural, or textual access. Explain the reason. These are not calibrated probabilities.
- When language uncertainty prevents judgment, leave the affected score unassigned. When a tentative score is useful, label it low confidence and keep the overall draft out of definitive rankings pending review.
- Never normalize one batch to manufacture spacing. Treat gaps of about two points or less as editorial close calls. This is a convention, not a statistical confidence interval. Explain the deciding tradeoff when the user needs a choice.
- Production feasibility and market fit may be discussed separately if requested. No score estimates box-office revenue, festival selection, or the probability of a hit.

## Revision comparison

Use the same rubric and evaluate the same source scope for each version. Explain each meaningful score change through changed scenes or relationships. If an earlier assessment was mistaken, name the corrected interpretation instead of attributing the entire change to the rewrite. Re-evaluate both drafts if a new rubric is required.
