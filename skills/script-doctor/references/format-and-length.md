# Format- and length-aware judgment

## Establish the dramatic unit

Use the author's stated format and target duration first. Record whether the unit is a standalone story, an episode, a pilot, a season, a series, an excerpt, or a treatment. A micro-drama can be standalone or serialized; runtime alone does not decide this. Identify episode order, series/season boundaries, and available supporting material. A series bible supplies intention and context, not evidence that unwritten episodes execute it.

Record target runtime when supplied. Otherwise give an estimated range with the reason, or state that runtime is unknown. Keep target length distinct from a timed reading or a performance estimate. Page count and word count describe the document, not reliable screen duration: formatting, script/language, pauses, action, songs, and montage vary. Do not apply a universal one-page-per-minute conversion or dialogue-speed rule. If length is uncertain, proceed with a stated qualitative lens and flag recommendations that depend on timing.

These are review lenses, not industry definitions or rigid duration thresholds:

| Work | Emphasis | Avoid imposing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Micro-drama / two-minute standalone | Quick but sufficient orientation, economical characterization, one legible central tension, consequential movement, compressed emotional preparation, an ending with meaning | A full biography, multiple subplots, a major personality transformation, a twist, or a feature beat sheet |
| Serialized micro-drama episode | A legible entry point given prior context, fresh progress or complication, consequences that carry forward, an earned reason to continue | Resetting the story every episode, an arbitrary cliffhanger at a fixed second, or complete resolution of the season in each installment |
| Short film | Scope proportional to its duration, selected details, controlled escalation or accumulation, a deliberate ending, room for the intended performance style | Padding toward a feature or treating every unresolved question as a defect |
| Feature | Sustained development, sequence progression, proportion, relationships and plot strands that justify their space, substantial preparation and payoff | Extra credit merely for length, cast size, or number of themes |
| Web-series / television pilot | An engaging episode, a clear invitation into the world, character relationships, and a plausible source of future story | Proving an entire season from a pilot or replacing present-tense drama with promises about later episodes |
| Continuing episode / anthology installment | Its local dramatic purpose, changes in knowledge or relationships, appropriate entry/exit points; for anthology work, the stated degree of independence | Judging an ordinary episode as if it must reintroduce the whole series, or assuming all series are serialized |
| Season / series | Cumulative choices and consequences, progression, continuity, distribution of focus, variation, thematic development, and payoffs at the stated boundary | Treating an average of episode scores as proof of series quality, or inventing later resolutions |

If the form is experimental, observational, comedic, or intentionally unresolved, identify its actual organizing principle. These lenses do not require constant conflict, a sympathetic lead, a three-act structure, or a cliffhanger.

## Apply the same fundamentals proportionally

Use the shared eight-category scorecard. Change what sufficient execution looks like within the work, not the weight of a category because the script is short.

- **Character:** In two minutes, a revealing choice, contradiction, or changed relationship may provide enough depth. In longer work, assess whether repeated choices deepen or merely restate that characterization.
- **Plot/conflict:** A single dilemma can be complete. Look for a meaningful change, consequence, discovery, or designed accumulation; do not demand several reversals or subplots.
- **Emotion:** A specific gesture or shared object may prepare a payoff in seconds. Assess whether the needed meaning is available, not how many pages established it. Missing preparation and economical preparation are different findings.
- **Structure/pacing:** Examine the share of available time used for orientation, development, and payoff. A long setup that leaves the crucial choice unintelligible is different from a deliberate slow observation. In a series, assess each episode's shape and the rhythm between episodes separately.
- **Dialogue:** Evaluate precision, voice, subtext, and silence within the available time. A silent script can succeed through deliberate nonverbal expression; lack of spoken dialogue alone is not a zero or an unassessed category.
- **Theme:** A small observation or tension can be sufficient in a micro-story. Do not demand a philosophical speech or the breadth of a full season.
- **Cinematic storytelling:** Inspect how behavior, objects, framing implied by the writing, action, and sound carry information efficiently. Assess vertical/mobile constraints only if the intended delivery warrants them.
- **Specificity:** Distinctive execution matters at every length. A familiar premise can still be precise and affecting; duration itself proves neither originality nor sophistication.

For a two-minute target, identify what can plausibly fit: speech, pauses, physical action, transitions, and the emotional turn. If there is a timing concern, name the crowded or underused beat and suggest a specific cut, compression, redistribution, or simplification. Do not prescribe arbitrary timecodes without a timed source. Use scene/line locators; label any suggested timing budget as a proposal, not measured runtime.

Never claim that a hook, cliffhanger, or shortened beat guarantees watch time, completion rate, virality, or platform success. Where viewer data exists, distinguish that evidence from the script-level hypothesis.

## Evaluate episodic work at two levels

Read every supplied episode in order and maintain a continuity ledger: character knowledge, wants, relationship changes, timeline, promises, setups/payoffs, unresolved questions, and what each installment adds. Repetition, recap, and renewed conflicts can serve a format; assess their function and cost rather than banning them.

For each episode, ask what changes locally and what is carried forward. Distinguish a purposeful unresolved thread from an omitted causal step or a payoff repeatedly deferred without development. A cliffhanger can be effective, but the episode should still earn its duration through progress, experience, or discovery appropriate to its form.

For the additional season/series synthesis, examine:

- The source of continuing stories: recurring situations, accumulating consequences, or a finite arc.
- Whether characters and relationships develop consistently across installments.
- Escalation or variation, repetitive episodes, and the distribution of major turns.
- Whether mysteries and promises are developed and paid off at the intended boundary.
- Continuity gaps and how one episode changes the reading of another.

Use an independent, evidence-based season/series score only when the complete declared unit is supplied and read with adequate confidence. Do not calculate it by averaging episode scores. An optional episode average may be shown as a descriptive statistic, clearly separated from the overall judgment.

A complete pilot or episode may receive its own score even without the rest of the series. If essential missing context prevents a category judgment, withhold that category under the normal rubric. A selected run of episodes receives coverage of those episodes and a qualified synthesis, not a whole-season verdict. If a series is ongoing, assess a clearly defined completed season or supplied arc without predicting its future conclusion.

## Compare and report fairly

Default to comparable groups such as two-minute standalone stories, serialized micro-episodes of similar target length, half-hour pilots, or complete features. The grouping is an editorial choice stated before ranking, not an automatic duration cutoff. Rank season/series assessments separately from individual episodes.

When the user explicitly wants one mixed-format ranking, explain that scores reflect success within each work's stated scope. Keep format, unit, and runtime visible; preserve the within-format comparisons. No per-minute score, duration bonus, percentile rescaling, or automatic reward for complexity is valid here.

Every individual PDF must name its format, unit, length/timing basis, applied expectations, and any timing-related revision. Keep a micro-drama report proportionate, usually concise, while retaining all substantive findings; do not pad it to a fixed report length or omit evidence to meet a page quota. Every episode gets its own PDF, even when all episodes came from one source file. A multi-episode review also gets a synthesis PDF. If later series analysis changes an episode's finding or score, refresh its Markdown and PDF before final delivery.
